## When to Use

Marketing copy for a page: what to lead with, the headline, the subhead, section text, calls to action, meta
description. Also "tighten this paragraph" or "fix this headline". For the offer itself, email, or a whole-page
visual design use another skill; for conversion problems that are structural rather than verbal use
`conversion-page-review`. Adapted from https://github.com/coreyhaines31/marketingskills (MIT, © 2025 Corey
Haines), whose diagnosis, three-question gate and decompose-and-lock steps come in turn from
https://github.com/retieedra-profound/skills-marketing-library (MIT).

## Procedure

1. **Gather what you do not have.** Read the project's notes on product and audience first if there are any; ask
   only for what is missing. You need: the page type and its ONE primary action; the ideal customer, their
   problem, their objections and the words they use for it; what is sold, what makes it different, the outcome
   it delivers, and proof (numbers, testimonials, cases); where visitors come from and what they already know.
2. **Diagnose the input in one line, with evidence, before writing.**
   - *A. Blank or generic* (a one-line ask, or company-speak with no point of view): build the argument from the
     brief. You may choose the angle, never the facts: no invented statistics, customers or results.
   - *B. A real voice is present* (an angle, an opinion, a recognisable way of writing, even if rough): restore
     it. Keep their argument and vocabulary; fix the buried lead, the vague lines and the structure.
   - *C. A point edit* ("fix this headline"): change only what was asked plus the minimum around it, and return the
     rest untouched.
   For B and C, read taste from the input: their vocabulary is a voice choice (do not upgrade "ship" to
   "deploy"), where they spent words is a priority, what they left out is a scope choice, their angle is the
   positioning. A writing sample beats the default voice.
3. **Decompose and lock.** Do not write the page in one pass. Split it into named units (headline, subhead,
   button, each section heading and body, proof lines). For each, draft two or three candidates, keep the best,
   fix its worst flaw and lock it. Lines that already work in a B draft are locked from the start. Read the
   assembled page once for flow.
4. **Gate every line with three questions.** Can the reader picture it? Can it be proven true or false? Could a
   competitor write this exact sentence (the swap test)? Three yes-answers: keep. Two: sharpen the weak side.
   One or none: rewrite from the facts, or flag `[NEED: differentiator]` or `[NEED: proof]` instead of inventing.
5. **Write plainly and specifically.** Clear beats clever. Say the customer's own words. Benefits over features
   (what it does versus what that means for them). "Cut weekly reporting from 4 hours to 15 minutes", not "save
   time". Active voice, simple words ("use", not "utilise"), no intensifiers, no exclamation marks. One idea per
   section. Ration the punch: one or two memorable moments per page, usually the headline and one section.
6. **Keep claims supportable.** Write only what the source material supports. When a requested claim is not
   supported, do not write it and do not silently drop it: name it, say why it falls short, offer the closest
   supportable version, and say what evidence would unlock the original (a review export, a written guarantee, a
   certificate, a citable study).
7. **Structure the page.** Above the fold: a headline that carries the single most important message, a subhead of
   one or two sentences that adds specificity, one primary button. Then: proof, the problem in the reader's terms,
   benefits (three to five), how it works (three or four steps), objections (questions, guarantees, comparisons),
   a final call that recaps the value. Buttons say what the reader gets: "Start my free trial", "Get the full
   checklist", not "Submit" or "Learn more".
8. **Keep the tells out.** Never write: "it's not X, it's Y" contrasts; lists of negations ("no setup, no
   templates, no waiting"); a finished claim followed by a trailing pile of clauses; a question answered by the
   writer ("The result? Three times faster."); stock openers ("in today's fast-paced world", "unlock the power
   of"); long dashes in headlines, subheads and short copy. State one real absence once, near the button. Replace
   "seamless", "robust" and "streamline" with the fact they stand for.
9. **Run the leave-it-alone test.** For each change ask: is it measurably better, or just different? Revert what you
   cannot justify. Then read every sentence over twenty words, and the page aloud.
10. **Deliver in the shape asked for.** A point edit: only the changed lines with a one-line reason each. A page:
    the copy by section, a short note on the key choices, two or three alternatives for the headline and the
    button, and the page title and meta description if relevant.

## Pitfalls

- Imposing your taste on a draft that already had a good angle.
- Inventing a number, a customer or a testimonial to make a line land.
- Replacing synonyms to dodge a tell; the pattern comes back in new words. Rewrite from the facts.
- A page of ten punchy lines, which reads as none and as machine-made.
- Clever headlines the reader must decode.
- Copy for several audiences at once, with no single action.

## Verification

- The reply states the diagnosis (A, B or C) with its evidence before the copy.
- Every claim traces to the brief or the product facts; any gap is marked `[NEED: ...]` with what would fill it.
- Every line passed the three questions, and none would work unchanged on a competitor's page.
- No banned pattern remains, and no stat or testimonial was made up.
- In a point edit, everything outside the requested lines is byte for byte as given.
